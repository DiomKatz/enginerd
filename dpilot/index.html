DPilot

Custom Flight Computer, Autonomy Stack & Field Ground Station
Research & Development

A home-built autonomy system for small fixed-wing aircraft: a custom ESP32 flight board and a Raspberry Pi Zero 2W companion computer, 50 Hz state estimation, long-range LoRa telemetry, and a handheld field computer for mission control. Now flying autonomous waypoint missions, with visual odometry research under way.

System Requirements & Development Status

🛡️ Regulatory Compliance

  • Drone Operations: Designed for compliance with local UAV/drone regulations (e.g., FAA Part 107 in the US, EASA in the EU). Operators are responsible for adhering to all applicable laws regarding flight altitude, airspace, and registration.
  • RF Transmission: LoRa modules operate in the 915MHz ISM band (US) or 868MHz (EU). Ensure operation within legal frequency and power limits as specified by the FCC (US) or local authorities. Maximum transmission power is set to comply with regional requirements.
  • Encryption: AES-128 encryption is used for secure communication, following best practices for data privacy and security.
  • Camera Use: Visual recording and AI camera features must comply with privacy and data protection laws. Obtain necessary permissions for video capture in public and private spaces.
  • PCB & Electronics: Custom PCB design follows RoHS and CE guidelines for electronic safety and environmental compliance.

Note: Always check and follow your local regulations for drone operation, RF transmission, and data collection. The DPilot system is intended for research and educational use.

System Architecture & Data Flow

The DPilot Flight Computer

DPilot splits the work between two onboard computers. A custom ESP32 flight board handles everything that has to happen on time, every time: it samples the sensors, stabilizes the aircraft when the pilot flies in assisted mode, drives the servos and motor, logs to its SD card, and runs the long-range radio link. A Raspberry Pi Zero 2W companion computer does the heavier thinking: it estimates where the aircraft is and where it is pointing, executes the mission, computes the guidance and flight-control commands, and records video with synchronized flight data.

The two exchange data 50 times per second. The pilot stays in charge throughout: a mode switch on the transmitter selects manual, assisted or autonomous flight, an output selector routes either the pilot's or the computer's signals to the servos, and the flight board hands control back to the pilot if the companion computer stops responding.

On the ground, a LoRa base station connects the aircraft to the DPilot field computer, or to a laptop running the desktop ground-control software, for mission planning, geofences, live telemetry and post-flight debriefs. Video and a live view are available over Wi-Fi.

The DPilot Base Station

The base station is DPilot's radio on the ground: a 3D-printed enclosure that clips onto the pilot's RC transmitter, so flight data stays in view without looking away from the aircraft. Inside is the same ESP32 LoRa board as on the aircraft, running the same firmware; with no IMU fitted, it starts up in base-station mode automatically.

It receives and decrypts the telemetry stream, follows the frequency-hopping link, and passes everything over USB to the field computer or a laptop, which in turn send mission commands up to the vehicle. Its own two displays show the flight at a glance: a colour TFT with speed, altitude and either an artificial horizon or a G-G acceleration diagram, and a small OLED with link health (latency, signal strength, packet counts, hopping channel) and the base GPS.

The DPilot Field Computer

The field computer is a handheld ground station that replaces the laptop at the flying field or on the quay. A Raspberry Pi Zero 2W with a 3.5-inch touchscreen sits in a 3D-printed case on the pilot's transmitter, with the base-station radio plugged in over USB. It runs headless, with no desktop, and draws its own touch interface:

  • STATUS: artificial horizon, speed, altitude, battery, GPS and link health
  • MISSION: pick a mission, send its geofence, start and stop
  • MAP: offline satellite imagery with geofences, waypoints and the live track; waypoints can be edited on the spot
  • CAL: bench calibration of the vehicle's flight board
  • DRONE: Wi-Fi setup and remote start/stop of the companion computer's software

Spoken call-outs announce mission start and stop, link loss and recovery, GPS loss, low battery and mode changes, so the pilot can keep their eyes on the vehicle. The field computer uses the same telemetry and command code as the desktop ground station, so the radio protocol is identical. Wi-Fi is used only on the ground, for setup; the mission itself runs over the LoRa link.
